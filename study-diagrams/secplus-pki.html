PKI: how a certificate gets trusted

Security+ (SY0-701). Read the left column top to bottom: who signs whom. The middle column is how a browser checks it. The right column is how you find out a certificate was cancelled early.

ISSUING · WHO SIGNS WHOM

Root CA
Like the state office that authorizes DMV branches
Top of trust. Its certificate is self-signed and sits in every browser's trust store. Kept offline.
signs ↓
Intermediate CA
The local DMV branch
Does the day-to-day signing. If it's compromised, revoke it without touching the root.
signs ↓
Your certificate
The license itself
Holds your public key, the names it covers, dates and the CA's signature. You got it by sending a CSR that holds your public key. Your private key never left your server.

TRUSTING · WHAT THE BROWSER CHECKS

1. Signature chain
Your certificate → intermediate → a root already in the trust store. A missing intermediate breaks the chain.
2. Name match
The site name must match the certificate. Wildcard *.example.com covers one level of subdomains. A SAN certificate lists several exact names, even other domains.
3. Dates
Not before / not after. An expired certificate fails even if nothing else is wrong.
4. Not revoked
Checked with a CRL or OCSP (right column).
Self-signed
Signed by its own key, so no trusted CA vouches for it. Browsers warn. Fine for a lab.

CHECKING · STILL GOOD?

CRL
The printed list of cancelled licenses
A signed list of revoked serial numbers. Clients download it every so often, so it can be a bit stale.
OCSP
Calling the DMV about one license
Asks about one certificate in real time.
OCSP stapling
Carrying a fresh signed note from the DMV
The server fetches a recent OCSP answer and attaches it to the TLS handshake. Faster and more private.
Pinning · Escrow · HSM
Pinning: the app accepts only one specific certificate or key. Key escrow: a trusted party keeps a copy of keys for recovery. HSM: tamper-resistant hardware that stores keys and uses them without letting them out.
The trap: the CSR carries the public key. Any answer that sends the private key to the CA is wrong.
Clue words: "real-time, one certificate" = OCSP · "server attaches status" = stapling · "list, downloaded" = CRL · "all subdomains" = wildcard · "several names" = SAN.
Why an offline root? If the root's private key leaks, every certificate under it is untrustworthy. Keeping it off the network and letting intermediates do the work limits the damage.
